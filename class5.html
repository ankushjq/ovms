Part 1: Power Math

What is Electrical Power? (Joule Heating)

Work and Wattage

**Electrical Power ($P$)** is the rate at which electrical energy is converted into another form (like heat, light, or mechanical motion). It is measured in **Watts (W)**, where 1 Watt equals 1 Joule per second.

Analogy: Think of voltage as water pressure and current as flow rate. Power is how much work a water wheel can do!

The Core Equation:
P = V × I
Power (W) = Voltage (V) × Current (A)

Alternate Formulas (Substituting Ohm's Law)

By combining $P = VI$ with Ohm's Law ($V = IR$), we can derive two other extremely useful power equations. These let us solve for power even if we don't know the voltage or current!

If you don't know V:
Substitute $V = IR$
$P = (IR)I = \mathbf{I^2R}$
If you don't know I:
Substitute $I = V/R$
$P = V(V/R) = \mathbf{V^2/R}$
💡 SciOly Tip: The $P = I^2R$ formula shows that if you double the current through a wire, the heat power dissipation **quadruples** ($2^2 = 4$). This is why electrical grids transmit power at ultra-high voltages to keep current low and prevent transmission line heat loss!
Part 1: Power Math

Electrical Energy & Time ($E = P \times t$)

Energy: Work Over Time

**Power** is the *rate* of doing work, but **Energy ($E$)** is the *total amount* of work done over a period of time.

Formula: E = P × t
E = Energy (Joules, J)
P = Power (Watts, W)
t = Time (Seconds, s)

Since 1 Watt = 1 Joule/sec, running a 10W LED bulb for 10 seconds consumes **100 Joules** of electrical energy.

The Kilowatt-Hour (kWh)

Joules are tiny! A typical home consumes millions of Joules per hour. Because of this, power utility companies use a much larger unit for billing: the **Kilowatt-Hour (kWh)**.

  • 1 kilowatt (kW) = 1,000 Watts
  • 1 Kilowatt-Hour is the energy consumed by running a 1,000W appliance for 1 hour.
  • Conversion: $1\text{ kWh} = 1,000\text{ W} \times 3,600\text{ seconds} = 3,600,000\text{ Joules}$ (3.6 Megajoules!)
Trap: Never multiply kilowatts by minutes! Always convert time into hours before calculating Kilowatt-Hours ($kWh$).
Part 1: Power Math

Utility Bill Math & Energy Audits

Billing Calculations

To calculate how much money it costs to run any electrical appliance, follow these three steps:

  1. Convert the appliance Wattage into Kilowatts (divide by 1,000).
  2. Multiply Kilowatts by the total operating time in Hours to get $kWh$.
  3. Multiply the total $kWh$ by the power rate (e.g., $\$0.15$ per $kWh$).
Formula:
$\text{Cost} = \left(\frac{\text{Watts}}{1000}\right) \times \text{Hours} \times \text{Rate per kWh}$

Typical Power Ratings

Appliance Wattage kW
LED Light Bulb 10 W 0.01 kW
Laptop Computer 50 W 0.05 kW
Microwave 1,000 W 1.00 kW
Space Heater 1,500 W 1.50 kW
Example problem: Run a 1.5 kW space heater for 4 hours at a rate of $15\phi$ per kWh.
$\text{Energy} = 1.5\text{ kW} \times 4\text{ hours} = 6\text{ kWh}$.
$\text{Cost} = 6\text{ kWh} \times \$0.15 = \$0.90$.
Part 2: Batteries

Inside a Battery: Chemical Voltaic Cells

The Chemical Electron Pump

A battery does not create charge—it acts like an **electron pump** using chemical reactions to force electrons through a circuit. It consists of:

  • Anode (-): The negative electrode where **oxidation** occurs, releasing electrons into the wire.
  • Cathode (+): The positive electrode where **reduction** occurs, accepting incoming electrons.
  • Electrolyte: The liquid or paste conductor (containing free ions) separating the electrodes.
🔑 Mnemonic: **AN OX** and **RED CAT** (Oxidation at the Anode, Reduction at the Cathode).

Voltaic Pile History & Materials

Invented by **Alessandro Volta** in 1800, the first battery stack (the Voltaic Pile) piled alternating discs of Zinc and Copper separated by cardboard soaked in saltwater.

The cell voltage is determined by the difference in **reduction potential** between the two metals. The further apart they are on the metal activity series, the higher the voltage!

Metal Standard Reduction Potentials:
- Silver (Ag): +0.80 V (Strong cathode)
- Copper (Cu): +0.34 V
- Iron (Fe): -0.44 V
- Zinc (Zn): -0.76 V (Strong anode)
Expected Cu-Zn Cell potential: 0.34V - (-0.76V) = 1.10 V.
Part 2: Batteries

Battery Configurations: Series vs. Parallel

Series Battery Path (Voltage Boost)

Connecting cells in **series** (positive terminal of one cell connected to negative of next) adds their chemical potentials directly.

$V_{total} = V_1 + V_2 + V_3 ...$
Capacity (Amp-hours, Ah) remains the same!

Example: Four 1.5V AA batteries connected in series yield a total voltage of **6.0V**, but retain the same capacity (e.g., 2,000 mAh).

Parallel Battery Path (Capacity Boost)

Connecting cells in **parallel** (positive connected to positive, negative to negative) creates multiple current paths.

$V_{total} = V_{single}$ (Voltage remains unchanged)
$\text{Capacity}_{total} = \text{Capacity}_1 + \text{Capacity}_2 + ...$

Example: Four 1.5V AA batteries connected in parallel output only **1.5V**, but quadruple the capacity to **8,000 mAh** (letting the circuit run four times longer!).

Part 3: Practical Lab Safety

Resistor Power Ratings: Preventing Burnouts

Resistor Power Capacity

Every physical resistor has a maximum **power rating** (typically $0.25\text{W}$, $0.5\text{W}$, or $1\text{W}$) which defines how much heat energy it can safely radiate away per second.

If a circuit forces a resistor to dissipate more power than its rating, the component will heat up rapidly.

Condition for Burnout:
$\mathbf{P_{actual} \ge P_{rating}}$

The Magic Smoke & Failures

When a resistor burns out:

  • It will smell metallic, turn black, and emit smoke.
  • The conductive carbon film burns away, creating an **open circuit** (infinite resistance, blocking current).
  • In some cases, it may short out or crack in half.
🧪 Safety First: Never touch a resistor in a live breadboard circuit if you smell burning! Turn off the power supply immediately.

Hands-On Activity

Lab Task: Designing for Power Safety

The Challenge: Safe Resistor Selection

In Division B laboratory events, you may be asked to choose a resistor that keeps current under a limit *while* ensuring you do not exceed its power rating. Let's design!

Materials Required:

- 9V Battery or adjustable DC breadboard power supply
- Multimeter (set to measure DC voltage/current)
- Resistors: $10\,\Omega$, $100\,\Omega$, and $330\,\Omega$ ($1/4\,\text{W}$ rated)

Let's perform calculations: What is the heat power dissipated when each resistor is hooked directly to a 9V battery?

Step-by-Step Task & Analysis

  1. Analyze the 330 Ω Resistor:
    $P = V^2 / R = 9^2 / 330 = 81 / 330 = 0.245\text{ W}$.
    Compare: $0.245\text{W} < 0.25\text{W}$ (Safe! Resistor will get slightly warm but won't blow).
  2. Analyze the 100 Ω Resistor:
    $P = V^2 / R = 9^2 / 100 = 81 / 100 = 0.81\text{ W}$.
    Compare: $0.81\text{W} > 0.25\text{W}$ (Dangerous! Over three times the rating. The resistor will burn and smoke).
  3. Analyze the 10 Ω Resistor:
    $P = 9^2 / 10 = 8.1\text{ W}$ (Instant blowout/fire risk! Never do this!).
⚠️ Teacher Supervision Required: Keep your hands away from the resistor body when applying power to verify calculations!
