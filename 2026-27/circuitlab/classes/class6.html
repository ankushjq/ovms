Part 1: Motors

DC Motors: Electric Flow to Mechanical Spin

Lorentz Force & Torque

A **Direct Current (DC) Motor** converts electrical energy into physical rotational motion. It relies on the **Lorentz Force**: when electric current flows through a wire loop located inside a magnetic field, the field pushes on the moving charges.

Lorentz Force Direction:
Use **Right-Hand Rule**: Point fingers in direction of current ($I$), curl them to magnetic field ($B$), thumb points to force ($F$).

Since current flows in opposite directions on the two sides of a loop, one side is pushed UP and the other is pushed DOWN, creating rotational torque.

Key Motor Components

  • Stator: The outer housing containing permanent magnets that provide a stationary magnetic field.
  • Rotor (Armature): The spinning central core wrapped in copper wire coils.
  • Split-Ring Commutator: The rotating metal ring split in half. It slides against the brushes to reverse current direction every $180^\circ$ of spin.
  • Brushes: Sliding carbon blocks that conduct current from the external power supply into the rotating commutator.
🔑 Mnemonic: The split-ring **Commutator** "commutes" (reverses) the current. Without it, the motor would spin half a turn, get stuck, and sit vibrating!
Part 2: Generators

Generators & Electromagnetic Induction

Faraday's Law of Induction

An **Electric Generator** is the exact reverse of a motor: it converts mechanical motion (like a spinning wind turbine or hand-crank) into electrical energy.

This depends on **Faraday's Law**: moving a magnet relative to a coil of wire (or rotating a coil inside a magnetic field) changes the magnetic field passing through the loop. This change induces an electromotive force (EMF / voltage) that drives current.

Faraday's Law Equation:
$\mathbf{V_{induced} = -N \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}}$
Voltage is proportional to number of turns ($N$) and speed of magnetic flux change ($\Delta \Phi / \Delta t$).

Lenz's Law (Opposing Change)

**Lenz's Law** explains the negative sign in Faraday's equation: the direction of an induced current will always produce its own magnetic field that *opposes* the change that created it.

This is a result of the **Conservation of Energy**. If the induced field did not oppose the change, it would pull the magnet in faster, creating infinite free energy!

🧪 Real-World Example: Drop a strong neodymium magnet down a copper pipe. Even though copper is non-magnetic, the moving field induces current loops in the pipe that create a magnetic force pushing UP, making the magnet fall in slow motion!
Part 2: Induction & Grid

Transformers & the AC Power Grid

How Transformers Work

A **Transformer** changes the voltage of alternating current (AC) using **mutual induction**. It consists of a primary coil and a secondary coil wound around a common iron core.

A changing current in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic field inside the iron core, which then induces a voltage in the secondary coil.

The Transformer Equation:
$\mathbf{\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}}$
Ratio of voltages equals the ratio of turns. Since energy is conserved ($P_{in} \approx P_{out}$), we have: $V_p I_p = V_s I_s$.

- **Step-Up**: $N_s > N_p \rightarrow$ Voltage increases, current decreases.
- **Step-Down**: $N_s < N_p \rightarrow$ Voltage decreases, current increases.

The DC Transformer Trap

Why only AC? Transformers *require* a constantly changing magnetic field to induce voltage.

⚠️ SciOly Test Trap: If you connect a transformer to a **Direct Current (DC)** source, the magnetic field is constant ($\Delta \Phi / \Delta t = 0$). No voltage will be induced in the secondary coil ($V_s = 0\text{V}$)!

AC Grid Transmission: Power lines lose power as heat ($P_{loss} = I^2R$). Grids use step-up transformers to boost voltage to hundreds of thousands of volts, dropping current ($I$) to near zero to minimize lines heating up!

Part 3: Environmental Sensors

Light Dependent Resistors (LDR) / Photoresistors

The Photoresistor Mechanism

An **LDR (Light Dependent Resistor)** is a variable resistor whose resistance changes according to light levels. It is made of a high-resistance semiconductor like **Cadmium Sulfide (CdS)**.

When photons (light packets) hit the LDR track, they break electrons free from their atoms. This creates free charge carriers, allowing current to flow more easily and reducing the resistance.

💡 Mnemonic: **"Light Down Resistance" (L.D.R.)**
- Dark = High Resistance (typically $100\text{ k}\Omega$ to $1\text{ M}\Omega$)
- Light = Low Resistance (typically $100\ \Omega$ to $1\text{ k}\Omega$)

Schematic Symbol & Uses

The LDR schematic symbol is a standard resistor rectangle (or zig-zag) enclosed in a circle with two arrows pointing inward (representing incoming light rays).

Common Applications:

  • Automatic streetlights that turn on at dusk.
  • Solar garden lights (sensing when the sun sets to activate the LED).
  • Camera exposure sensors.
💡 SciOly Test Trap: If an LDR is placed in a voltage divider circuit, make sure to read if it is wired to the high side or low side. The output voltage will change in opposite directions as light levels swing!
Part 3: Environmental Sensors

Thermistors: Thermal Resistors

NTC vs. PTC Curves

A **Thermistor** is a thermally sensitive resistor whose resistance varies dramatically with temperature. Unlike normal metal conductors (which increase resistance slightly when heated), thermistors are custom metal oxide semiconductors with two behaviors:

  • NTC (Negative Temperature Coefficient): Temperature UP $\rightarrow$ Resistance DOWN. This is the most common temperature sensor.
  • PTC (Positive Temperature Coefficient): Temperature UP $\rightarrow$ Resistance UP. Often used as a self-resetting overcurrent fuse!

Practical Features & Applications

Thermistors have highly non-linear curves (resistance changes exponentially with temperature).

Common Applications:

  • Digital clinical thermometers.
  • Engine coolant temperature sensors in cars.
  • In-rush current limiters in power supplies.
  • Battery pack temperature safety monitors.
🔑 Quick Check: If you blow hot air onto an NTC thermistor, what happens to the current in the loop? Because temp goes up, resistance goes *down*, meaning the current in the loop will *increase*!

Potentiometers: 3-Terminal Variable Dividers

The Wiper Mechanism

A **Potentiometer** is a three-terminal component. Pins 1 and 3 are connected to the two ends of a fixed internal carbon resistive track. Pin 2 is connected to a sliding contact called the **wiper**, which moves when you turn the dial.

If you measure resistance between Pin 1 and Pin 3, it is always constant (e.g. $10\text{ k}\Omega$).

When you spin the dial, you change the ratio of the two internal resistor halves:

$R_{12} + R_{23} = R_{13}$ (Total Resistance is constant)
Turning clockwise increases $R_{12}$ and decreases $R_{23}$.

The Voltage Divider Formula

If you connect Pin 1 to a $5\text{V}$ power source, and Pin 3 to Ground ($0\text{V}$), the center wiper pin (Pin 2) acts as a dynamic **voltage divider**. Its output voltage changes smoothly from $0\text{V}$ to $5\text{V}$ as you spin the dial!

$$V_{out} = V_{in} \times \frac{R_{23}}{R_{12} + R_{23}} = V_{in} \times \frac{R_{23}}{R_{total}}$$
Test Trap / Setup Rule: To use a potentiometer as a simple 2-terminal variable resistor (rheostat), you *must* wire the wiper (Pin 2) to one of the outer pins. Leaving the center pin floating makes the dial useless!

Part 4: Lab Competition Prep

Competition Mastery: Cracking Mock Lab Stations

The "Mystery Box" Challenge

A standard Science Olympiad practical station gives you a cardboard box with three external banana jack terminals (Labeled $A, B, C$) with a hidden component inside. You must use a multimeter to determine what is inside:

  • Resistor: Measures the same resistance value in both directions ($A\rightarrow B$ and $B\rightarrow A$).
  • Diode/LED: Measures extremely low resistance one way (Forward Bias), and infinite resistance when DMM probes are reversed (Reverse Bias).
  • Capacitor: Resistance starts low (charging) and climbs to infinity ($OL$).
  • Open Circuit: Displays $OL$ (overload/infinity) in both directions.

Troubleshooting Breadboard Faults

If your target circuit fails to function during a lab test, run these quick sanity checks under time pressure:

  1. Check Power Rails: Measure voltage across the power bus bars. If it reads $0\text{V}$, there is a **short circuit** directly bridging power to ground!
  2. Polarity Errors: Look closely at diodes, LEDs, and capacitors. Are their anodes (longer pins) oriented towards the positive voltage source?
  3. Floating Contacts: Verify that pins reside in the exact same 5-pin vertical columns. Pushing wires into adjacent columns creates open circuits!
Hands-On Task

Hands-On: Calibrating a Potentiometer Divider

Step-by-Step Calibration Lab

In this task, we will wire a $10\text{ k}\Omega$ rotary potentiometer as a voltage divider and calibrate its physical dial position to produce exactly $2.50\text{ V}$ from a $5.00\text{ V}$ supply.

🛠️ Required Materials:
  • Solderless Breadboard & Jumper Wires
  • $10\text{ k}\Omega$ Potentiometer
  • $5\text{V}$ Power Source (or $5\text{V}$ regulator rail)
  • Digital Multimeter (DMM)
📝 Setup Instructions:
  1. Plug the three pins of the potentiometer into separate vertical rows on your breadboard.
  2. Connect Pin 1 of the potentiometer to the $5\text{V}$ red power rail using a jumper.
  3. Connect Pin 3 of the potentiometer to the ground blue rail using a jumper.
  4. Turn your DMM dial to **DC Volts (V)**. Connect the red probe to Pin 2 (center wiper pin) and the black probe to the ground rail.
  5. Carefully turn the potentiometer dial until the multimeter reads exactly $2.50\text{ V}$.

The Mathematics Check

Since $V_{in} = 5.0\text{V}$ and we want $V_{out} = 2.5\text{V}$, let's use the voltage divider equation to find the internal resistances:

$V_{out} = V_{in} \times \frac{R_{23}}{R_{total}}$
$2.50\text{ V} = 5.00\text{ V} \times \frac{R_{23}}{10\text{ k}\Omega}$
$\frac{2.50}{5.00} = \frac{R_{23}}{10\text{ k}\Omega}$
$0.50 = \frac{R_{23}}{10\text{ k}\Omega}$
$R_{23} = 5.0\text{ k}\Omega$

Since $R_{12} + R_{23} = 10\text{ k}\Omega$, if $R_{23} = 5.0\text{ k}\Omega$, then $R_{12}$ must also equal $5.0\text{ k}\Omega$. The potentiometer is split exactly in half!

Troubleshooting Tip: If your DMM display reads negative voltages (e.g. $-2.50\text{V}$), check your probes. You have plugged the DMM positive probe to ground and black probe to the wiper, or vice-versa!
